Abstract

Reinforcement learning (RL) can improve sensorimotor driving policies, but closed-loop training in a photorealistic simulator has remained impractical: rollouts are expensive and small-batch off-policy optimization is unstable. We introduce Greg, a reinforcement fine-tuning framework that stabilizes it. Greg formulates driving as a constrained Markov decision process with a logically structured reward and augmented Lagrangian constraint handling, trains distributional critic ensembles whose disagreement prioritizes a stratified replay buffer, and expands both the reward and the cost value target through a recurrent latent model that takes no RL gradient. Fine-tuning an imitation-learned policy with Greg reaches a 92.0 driving score (DS) on Bench2Drive, level with the strongest published camera-only policy, at 27FPS and within 480 GPU hours on two RTX 3090 GPUs, from camera images to steering and acceleration with no high-definition map and no PID controller in between. The advantage widens on adversarial scenarios that expert demonstrations never contain: 54.2 DS against 27.9 for the strongest imitation baseline, with no collisions. Those situations have to be handled by reacting, and demonstrations of clean driving contain no examples of that.

Overview

We introduce a state-of-the-art sensorimotor driving model, fine-tuned in closed-loop with efficient model-free RL. This project page collects the material the paper has no room for, and does not repeat any table the paper already contains. First, we give the full implementation details: the reward design, the network architecture, the training procedure and hyperparameters, and the evaluated baselines (Sec. S1). Second, we report additional ablations and evaluations that are not in the paper, including the per-term reward ablation, the comparison against published reward formulations, Town05 Short, and action alignment on real driving video (Sec. S2). Third, we discuss additional qualitative results, including failure cases (Sec. S3). The video above provides additional rollout visualizations.

Despite promising results in an area dominated by large, privileged IL planning-based methods, our findings highlight the inherent difficulty of stabilizing RL for high-dimensional visual control settings. Although we take several steps to mitigate these challenges, including an effective replay buffer and critic ensemble, stable RL from vision-based embeddings remains difficult. Code and model checkpoints are available from this page.

S1.1Reward Design

This section defines our reward function and contrasts it with prior designs [3, 4].

Our reward is designed to balance multiple task objectives while promoting effective exploration in urban environments with bottleneck and safety-critical states. A comparison of reward functions in urban driving literature can be found in Table S1. While we leverage a simpler reward function than some prior work, such as GigaFlow [3], we introduce explicit constraints and exploration terms. Our task reward is defined as $r_{\text{ext}}=\sqrt[3]{r_{\text{RC}}\cdot r_{\text{LF}}\cdot r_{\text{Speed}}}$, the geometric mean of route completion $r_{\text{RC}}$, lane following $r_{\text{LF}}$, and speed $r_{\text{Speed}}$. This formulation enforces balanced behavior across objectives, since poor performance in any single term sharply reduces the overall reward. The coefficient $\lambda_{\text{int}}$ is a scalar hyperparameter set to 0.1.

Route Completion. $r_{\text{RC}} = \rho_0 + (1-\rho_0)\,\mathrm{clip}(\Delta_t/\Delta_{\text{ref}},0,1)$ is an instantaneous reward computed from the per-step progress $\Delta_t$ made along the route toward the goal. It is a clamped linear ramp from a small floor $\rho_0$ at standstill to $1$ at the reference progress $\Delta_{\text{ref}}$ that saturates it, so it lies in $[0,1]$ by construction.

Lane Following. $r_{\text{LF}} = \prod_{k} (1 - 0.9\,\mathbb{1}_k)$ encourages lane discipline as a product over six indicators $\mathbb{1}_k$: lane invasion, driving the wrong way, running a red light, running a stop sign, following too closely, and lateral offset from the lane center. Each violated indicator multiplies the term by $0.1$ rather than zeroing it, so a single infraction dominates without erasing the gradient, and the product lies in $[0,1]$.

Speed Reward. $r_{\text{Speed}} = \exp\!\big(-(\mathrm{max}(v_{\text{current}} - v_{\text{desired}},0)/\sigma_v)^2\big)$ falls off with the squared over-speed relative to the desired speed, so travelling at or below the desired speed is not penalized by this term and it lies in $[0,1]$. All three task terms therefore lie in $[0,1]$, which is what makes their geometric mean well defined and keeps $r_{\text{ext}}$ in $[0,1]$ as well.

Intrinsic Exploration Reward. We do not use forward-dynamics curiosity [5, 4] for the exploration bonus, since predicting future observations in dense, interactive urban environments is itself difficult and adds computational overhead [6, 4, 7]. In our preliminary experiments the resulting bonus tracked stochastic visual variation more than novelty, which is the failure mode random network distillation is designed to avoid [8].

Instead, our model-free formulation utilizes random network distillation (RND) [8] to generate a stable intrinsic exploration reward. RND measures a prediction error against a deterministic representation, thus providing a simple and stable exploration signal. Specifically, a fixed randomly initialized network assigns a target embedding to every state. A separate predictor network is continuously updated to learn to output this target embedding obtained by the random net. Because the predictor trains exclusively on data the agent actively collects, its accuracy improves solely on familiar, frequently visited states. When the agent experiences a completely new situation, the untrained predictor produces a high error which directly translates into a positive intrinsic reward:

\begin{equation} r_{\text{int}}= \| g_\phi(\mathbf{z}) - g_{\text{RND}}(\mathbf{z}) \|_2^2 \label{eq:rnd} \end{equation}

where $g_\phi$ is the predictor and $g_{\text{RND}}$ is the fixed target network. We also note that this approach is far more effective than simple autoencoding, which typically wastes capacity trying to reconstruct irrelevant pixel-level details. Since the RND target is deterministic, unpredictable visual noise cannot artificially inflate the exploration reward. As the agent drives, the predictor becomes increasingly accurate at recognizing previously explored areas, causing the reward to naturally fade for common scenarios. We find that this mechanism encourages the agent to discover rare, safety-relevant events without overemphasizing standard expert trajectories, which in turn leads to significantly faster convergence (nearly halving the training time).

Cost Terms. We propose to explicitly separate safety critical terms as dedicated cost targets for the critic, which cleanly reflects the difference between standard task rewards and safety boundaries. The infraction cost $c$ uses a worst-case aggregation to represent the most severe violation at each step, $c = \max(c_{\text{TTC}}, c_{\text{term}})$. The dense term $c_{\text{TTC}}$ is a bounded, monotone decreasing map of the time to collision, and the time-to-collision signal itself is already the minimum over surrounding actors, the red light, the stop sign, and map geometry, so the most imminent threat among dynamic obstacles and static traffic controls is selected before the cost is formed. This extends CaRL [9], which incorporates TTC but omits the red light and stop sign terms. The terminal term $c_{\text{term}}$ fires on any cause that ends the episode, whether a collision, a red light or stop sign violation, or the vehicle ending up blocked, and at a terminal the stored cost label additionally carries the absorbing mass $1+1/(1-\gamma_c)$, which is what puts a discounted cost on the order of hundreds rather than units. Aggregating by a maximum rather than a sum prevents the agent from diluting one severe hazard across several mild ones.

Comparing With Other Rewards. Many prior RL driving approaches rely on additive or monolithic reward formulations. In additive designs, the agent may exploit imbalances between reward terms, prioritizing easier objectives while neglecting others. We thus follow a multiplicative structure which ensures that no objective can be easily ignored during training. CaRL [9] makes a similar observation, but uses weaker penalties for safety-related terms (e.g., collisions and lane violations). As a result, training takes longer to stabilize. We further find that terminating episodes for off-road violations is unnecessary and instead incorporate a different route-completion signal, $r_{\text{LF}}$.

Our approach entirely replaces arbitrary scalar penalties with the strict infraction cost $c$. By formalizing safety limits as explicit CMDP constraints rather than negotiable rewards, our formulation provides a principled way to evaluate infractions without requiring extensive coefficient tuning.

Think2Drive [11] and Raw2Drive [4] (our nearest baseline) inherit several limitations of additive reward formulations, which is a widespread design [13, 14, 3]. These also lack a well-shaped terminal penalty. Without a strong termination cost, the agent struggles to learn to avoid unsafe behaviors (as demonstrated in our analysis, Sec. S2.2). While these methods partially compensate for this limitation through large-scale parallel environments and sophisticated world models, we instead encode safety directly in the objective. Additionally, Raw2Drive adopts a categorical action head, which substantially expands the action space and reduces sample efficiency, whereas our formulation maintains a compact action space. While this design choice may seem like a minor point, it has a large impact in practice, substantially improving sample efficiency and easing RL. Next, we situate our approach within the broader literature.

Reward Transferability Beyond CARLA. Our reward formulation relies on a small set of interpretable signals (e.g., route progress, lane adherence, and collision risk). Such signals are not tailored to CARLA, but are commonly assumed in real-world autonomous driving systems, i.e., can be estimated from onboard sensors and lightweight perception modules [15]. For instance, vehicle speed is directly available from the CAN bus, while lane boundaries, traffic lights, and nearby objects can be detected from camera inputs, and the route itself can be obtained from a standard navigation service [16] (further discussed in Sec. S2). In contrast, several competitive RL baselines, e.g., GigaFlow [3], rely on more complex reward formulations and privileged 3D state inputs. Similarly, many strong IL baselines (e.g., TransFuser [17], TF variants [18], and HiP-AD [19]) depend on dense auxiliary supervision such as detection, motion forecasting, mapping, and planning losses. In our evaluations, we also demonstrate generalization to other environments, e.g., on HUGSIM [20].

Learning the Reward Instead. Our formulation uses a small set of interpretable and controllable signals that allow direct inspection and control of agent behavior during training. One alternative is to learn the reward function directly from demonstrations using inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) or related reward learning approaches [21, 13, 22]. However, reward optimization remains relatively nascent for complex driving tasks, where nearly all competitive approaches instead rely on planning-based IL formulations [23].

IRL methods may require large and diverse expert datasets to recover meaningful reward functions [24], and learned rewards are typically difficult to interpret and control. This can hinder the diagnosis of failure cases or the explicit adjustment of safe behaviors during training. A related line of recent work explores reward design via large language or vision-language models, i.e., to generate rewards or preference signals [25]. However, these often also incorporate various simplifications, such as involving a human in the loop.

Ultimately, we believe IL, IRL, and RL approaches are inherently complementary for realizing robust, generalized real-world driving: interpretable rewards can provide stability and controllability in safety-critical settings, while learned or language-guided rewards may help discover additional behavioral signals or provide richer shaping where manual specification is difficult. Thus, while specifying reward terms directly can be intuitive and efficient in our settings, future work may explore integrating such learned rewards. This remains understudied for high-dimensional urban driving tasks [13].

S1.2Model Architecture

While most state-of-the-art baselines involve planning-based approaches, we instead highlight the benefits of our reinforcement fine-tuning approach by revisiting the lightweight TCP architecture [1]. This allows us to isolate the impact of RL and assess how far reinforcement fine-tuning alone can push performance, i.e., even under poor initialization. Specifically, we analyze TCP-Ctrl, the control-based variant, which significantly underperforms the waypoint-based TCP-Traj model. We discuss these architectures, as well as upgrades, in detail next.

Lightweight Network Architecture (TCP-Ctrl). TCP is a camera-based model with a two-branch output structure: TCP-Traj predicts waypoints executed by manually tuned PID controllers, while TCP-Ctrl directly outputs low-level controls. The original work heuristically ensembles the two. Recent work shows that TCP-Traj is highly competitive and outperforms both TCP-Ctrl and the heuristically tuned TCP ensemble on Bench2Drive [7]. The relatively weak performance of TCP-Ctrl therefore makes it a natural choice for measuring the benefits of our method in the context of prior research and common architectures. We demonstrate that state-of-the-art driving performance can be achieved with a simple, non-ensemble TCP-Ctrl model trained using the proposed approach, outperforming several strong recent baselines (e.g., ReCogDrive [26], Raw2Drive [4], Hydra-Next [27]), DriveTransformer [28]) on Bench2Drive.

TCP Model Backbone. Following Wu et al. [1], we employ ResNet-34 [29] as the image encoder. The input image is $900\times256$ (concatenated across views), each camera has a $70^\circ$ FOV [30], and the remaining measurements (speed and navigation goal) are encoded with an MLP of two fully connected layers of width 128 with ReLU activations. The two embeddings are then concatenated to form $\bz$. The image backbone is initialized from ImageNet [31]. While we do not modify the architecture itself, we show that two straightforward changes: adding a rear-view image, placed in the top right of the front image, and modifying the output space (Sec. III-A of the main paper), increase TCP-Ctrl performance from 30.47 to 69.19 DS on Bench2Drive, with most of the gain attributed to the hybrid-action output modification. We refer to this variant as TCP-Ctrl+.

DINOv3 + Transformer Variant. Two minimal changes to TCP-Ctrl, a rear-view input and the hybrid action head, followed by reinforcement fine-tuning, already carry the original 30.47 DS baseline to 78.11 DS on Bench2Drive at unchanged 100FPS inference. To check that the framework does not depend on that particular backbone, we also evaluate a stronger architectural variant: a frozen DINOv3 backbone with a multi-modal transformer head, which enlarges spatial reasoning and control capacity. We replace the ResNet backbone with a frozen DINOv3 [2] vision encoder and introduce a transformer prediction head [32]. To fully leverage the $70^\circ$ FOV from all six surround-view cameras [30], we replace the rear-view inset approach with a dense $2 \times 3$ image mosaic. The cameras are arranged into a single $512 \times 896$ resolution input. We process this mosaic using a multi-branch ViT-Split architecture [33] that fuses early, late, and trainable twin-branch features. This fundamentally improves the adaptation efficiency of the massive vision backbone ($\sim$300M parameters) while preserving strong zero-shot spatial representations (projecting the mosaic into a $32 \times 56$ spatial feature grid). Speed and navigation goals are encoded via an MLP with GELU [34] activations and LayerNorm [35] to serve as state queries. The transformer head comprises a 3-layer, 8-head cross-attention decoder utilizing pre-LayerNorm, allowing these state and temporal queries to explicitly attend to the 2D spatial memory.

Context Aggregation and Control Head. Both model variants share a similar control goal: mapping fused visual and state representations into the hybrid control space. This space combines a continuous steering value, modeled by a Beta distribution, with a categorical acceleration command. We intentionally retain this hybrid approach as we find prior RL methods which fully discretize the action space, such as Raw2Drive [4], degrade driving performance by complicating exploration and temporal credit assignment during training. In both architectures, the acceleration head predicts a 6-class categorical command to capture fine-grained braking, coasting, and throttle modes.

The original TCP-Ctrl baseline relies on explicit feature concatenation and standard MLPs for control prediction. Visual and measurement features are concatenated, yielding a 640-dimensional vector (512 visual + 128 state), and passed through a fusion MLP with ReLU activations to produce a 256-dimensional state vector. The subsequent control head processes this vector through a standard MLP comprising two fully connected layers of width 256 with ReLU activations. The final output layers predict the steering concentration parameters $\zeta_1$ and $\zeta_2$ using a Softplus activation.

Our stronger DINOv3 + transformer variant leverages cross-attention and a deeper residual architecture to improve feature fusion and training stability. The Transformer [32] cross-attention mechanism replaces explicit feature concatenation, naturally yielding a unified 512-dimensional context token. To prevent vanishing gradients during fine-tuning, this token is first normalized via LayerNorm and projected to 256 dimensions. It is then processed through two 256-dimensional residual blocks, each consisting of two linear layers, GELU activations, Dropout, and LayerNorm, followed by a final 256-dimensional linear layer with GELU.

Critics and Target Nets. The reward and cost critics utilize an ensembled, distributional architecture to ensure stable and conservative value estimation. Both objectives share identical critic structures that operate on the latent embedding $\bz$ produced by the perception backbone and fusion module. The state-action input is formed by concatenating $\bz$ with the action vector $\ba$ predicted by the policy. Each critic consists of a 5-layer (512-dimensional) MLP followed by a linear output layer. Rather than predicting the parameters of a constrained Gaussian distribution, the output layer computes a discrete set of $N$ quantiles to non-parametrically model the full return distribution (see Sec. S1.3 for complete training details). To accurately quantify epistemic uncertainty and prevent errors in value estimation, we maintain an ensemble of $K=10$ independent critics for both the reward and cost objectives. Each critic is paired with a corresponding target network of identical architecture.

Intrinsic Module. We add curiosity-signal supervision via a lightweight intrinsic-reward module. Instead of leveraging a next-state prediction objective, which can be difficult to model in dense urban scenes, we adopt RND [8]. The module operates on the latent embedding $\bz$ produced by the perception backbone. Specifically, we use two small MLPs: a fixed, randomly initialized target network and a trainable predictor network. Both networks share the same architecture, consisting of two fully connected 512-dimensional layers with ReLU activations followed by a linear output layer that produces a compact embedding. The target network remains frozen throughout training, while the predictor network learns to regress the target embedding.

World Model. The recurrent latent model is an auxiliary module attached to the same embedding stream, and its full specification is deferred to Sec. S1.4. Here we record only how it connects to the networks above. Its input is the 522-dimensional vector $\be_t = [\bz_t, \brho_t]$, formed from the 512-dimensional encoder embedding and 10 route scalars, and its transition is conditioned on the 7-dimensional executed action, i.e., the steering scalar and the six-way acceleration one-hot. All of its internal MLPs are 512-dimensional with LayerNorm and GELU activations. Its belief $\bb_t = [\bh_t, \bxi_t] \in \mathbb{R}^{512}$ is read by its own prediction heads, by the imagination rollout of Sec. S1.4, and by the actor and the critics: the critics concatenate it to their state input, and the actor receives it through a zero-initialized residual on the input latent, so the module is an exact no-op at initialization and the imitation warm start is undisturbed. The belief is detached in both places, so the RL losses never backpropagate into the model, and the model is trained on its own objective alone.

Given our two model architecture variants, we next outline the training process. Our simple, single-frame architectures achieve strong performance on Bench2Drive without requiring complex multi-stage planning, temporal history, or exhaustive controller tuning, as in comparative state-of-the-art methods.

S1.3Training

Vision-based, model-free RL policies remain notoriously difficult to optimize [36, 37, 38]. We thus find a need to comprehensively address various aspects of learning failure through multiple regularization mechanisms. We do not rely on large-scale compute and abstract, lower-dimensional state representations as in prior work [3]. Instead, our policy operates on high-dimensional visual embeddings, making training efficiency essential.

We initially build on prior work in robotics and optimization that constrains the policy space during training [39, 40]. While this improves stability, we found it insufficient to reliably learn viable policies. In fact, Fig. 3 of the main paper shows that training still degrades performance relative to the initialized model. To further mitigate training issues, we introduce a stratified uncertainty prioritized replay buffer together with a critic ensemble. The complete training and hyperparameter details are described below, with an outline of the training process shown in Alg. S1.

Behavior Cloning Initialization. We follow the standard Bench2Drive training split [30], which contains roughly five hours of driving data, and initialize our model from expert demonstrations (a widespread practice [41, 42]). This follows the TCP-Ctrl baseline, which provides a poor initialization (30.47 DS). In our improved version of TCP-Ctrl+, we retain the Beta head from TCP for steering but use a categorical head for acceleration, which simplifies the acceleration action space while preserving a sufficiently expressive representation (adding the rear-view to this model, and keeping the original action space only upgrades from 30 to 35 DS). This action space choice is motivated by two observations: (1) acceleration often requires exploring extreme values, e.g., full braking, and the Beta distribution, while effective for smooth values in $[0,1]$, is less suited for sharply representing these edges;

and (2) acceleration can be approximated via a discrete set rather than a finely resolved continuous range. This significantly outperforms the original TCP-Ctrl branch, particularly in challenging scenarios where the tuned TCP-merged network continues to struggle. We also observe substantial forgetting during RL training; for example, Fig. 3 of the main paper shows a DS of 38.40 at the first stages of training when using vanilla SAC.

Critic Ensembles. In the CMDP formulation, we train two distinct distributional critic ensembles, for the reward and cost. We leverage ensemble double $Q$-learning-based update strategy [43]. We maintain two ensembles each of $K$ critics: $\{Q_{\boldsymbol{\psi}_k}^r\}_{k=1}^K$ for the task reward and $\{Q_{\boldsymbol{\psi}_k}^c\}_{k=1}^K$ for the safety cost.

For any training batch, we compute the target value using a randomly sampled subset of $M=2$ critics.

A key distinction of our approach is that each critic $Q_{\boldsymbol{\psi}_k}$ is distributional; rather than assuming a parameterized Gaussian form, it employs Quantile Regression to output a discrete set of $N$ quantiles that non-parametrically model the full return distribution. This formulation allows the model to capture aleatoric uncertainty, or inherent environmental stochasticity, via the variance across the predicted quantiles for a given state-action pair. Concurrently, the disagreement between critics in the ensemble, measured by the variance of their expected values, captures epistemic uncertainty. We note that this is a departure from standard SAC [44], which uses only two critics, as well as standard CMDP approaches which often do not model uncertainty in a distributional manner. We leverage the uncertainty to compute a regularized objective as well as prioritize samples in the replay buffer.

Epistemic and Aleatoric Uncertainty. Both the regularized actor objective below and the replay priorities of the following paragraph read their uncertainty from the same two ensembles, so we define it once here. Each critic $k$ outputs a discrete set of $N$ quantiles, written $\{q_{k,i}^c\}_{i=1}^N$ for the cost and $\{q_{k,i}^r\}_{i=1}^N$ for the reward; these are the quantities the main paper denotes $q_{i,\boldsymbol{\psi}_k}$, abbreviated here to keep the nested sums readable. The expected value of a single cost critic is the average of its quantiles, $\mu_k^c(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N q_{k,i}^c$.

Cost uncertainty is decomposed into aleatoric and epistemic components. Aleatoric cost uncertainty $U_{\text{aleatoric}}^c$ captures the inherent environmental randomness and is computed as the square root of the average variance across the predicted quantiles of the ensemble:

\begin{equation} U_{\text{aleatoric}}^c(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left( q_{k,i}^c(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) - \mu_k^c(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) \right)^2 \right) } \label{eq:aleatoric_cost} \end{equation}

Epistemic cost uncertainty $U_{\text{epistemic}}^c$ captures model ignorance and is calculated as the standard deviation of the expected values across the $K$ critics, where $\bar{\mu}^c(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \mu_k^c(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a})$ is the ensemble average mean:

\begin{equation} U_{\text{epistemic}}^c(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \left( \mu_k^c(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) - \bar{\mu}^c(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) \right)^2} \label{eq:epistemic_cost} \end{equation}

Symmetrically, we compute aleatoric reward uncertainty $U_{\text{aleatoric}}^r$ and epistemic reward uncertainty $U_{\text{epistemic}}^r$ from the reward ensemble's quantiles $\{q_{k,i}^r\}_{i=1}^N$:

\begin{equation} U_{\text{aleatoric}}^r(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left( q_{k,i}^r(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) - \mu_k^r(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) \right)^2 \right) } \label{eq:aleatoric_reward} \end{equation}
\begin{equation} U_{\text{epistemic}}^r(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \left( \mu_k^r(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) - \bar{\mu}^r(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) \right)^2} \label{eq:epistemic_reward} \end{equation}

The epistemic term of Eq. \(\ref{eq:epistemic_cost}\) and Eq. \(\ref{eq:epistemic_reward}\) is what the replay priorities of the following paragraph are built from: states the ensemble has not yet resolved are worth revisiting, while states whose spread is merely environmental noise are not.

Uncertainty-Regularized SAC Fine-Tuning. In our setup, vanilla SAC alone does not improve over the IL initialization. Both regularization and the AL objective are necessary; removing either results in poor performance. Nonetheless, once stabilized with our integrated framework, we observe strong generalization performance, e.g., across benchmarks and even in sim-to-real settings (Sec. S2).

Uncertainty-regularized SAC fine-tuning prevents the rapid forgetting of pretrained policies typical of standard off-policy algorithms. While SAC is an efficient off-policy algorithm, off-policy bootstrapping on outdated or low-quality transitions can amplify value estimation errors, destabilize both critic and actor updates, and degrade stable driving behaviors inherited from the expert initialization before RL signals can improve upon them. We therefore integrate an uncertainty-regularized SAC fine-tuning phase: we leverage the ensemble of distributional critic networks together with an augmented Lagrangian (AL) [40] objective to provide more stable, conservative updates for the actor.

The actor objective $\mathcal{J}(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$ is modified to maximize a pessimistic reward bound while satisfying a cost constraint using the AL formulation:

\begin{equation} \begin{split} \mathcal{J}(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) = \mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{D} \\ \mathbf{a} \sim \pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z})}} \Big[ & \cL_{\text{LCB}} - \cL_{\text{AL}}^c(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) - \alpha \log \pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{z}) \\ & - \beta D_{KL}(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}) \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{IL}}(\mathbf{z})) \Big] \end{split} \label{eq:actor_objective_supp} \end{equation}

where we slightly abuse notation and use $\bz$ as the policy input for brevity. Here, $\cL_{\text{LCB}} = \left(\bar{\mu}^r(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) - \lambda_r U_{\text{epistemic}}^r(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a})\right)$ is the lower confidence bound. $\cL_{\text{AL}}^c$ is the AL cost, which includes a quadratic penalty for constraint violations. First, we define the violation $V(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a})$ using the upper confidence bound (UCB) of the cost relative to a threshold $c_{\text{max}}$:

\begin{equation} V(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) = \left(\bar{\mu}^c(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) + \lambda_c U_{\text{epistemic}}^c(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a})\right) - c_{\text{max}} \label{eq:violation} \end{equation}

Given the violation of Eq. \(\ref{eq:violation}\), the total AL cost penalty $\cL_{\text{AL}}^c$ is:

\begin{equation} \cL_{\text{AL}}^c(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) = \nu V(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}) + \frac{\rho_{\text{AL}}}{2} \left(\max(0, V(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{a}))\right)^2 \label{eq:al^cost} \end{equation}

The expectation in Eq. \(\ref{eq:actor_objective_supp}\) is taken over latent observation embeddings $\mathbf{z}$ from the replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$ and actions $\mathbf{a}$ sampled from the current policy $\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$. The policy maximizes the LCB for reward (using $\bar{\mu}^r$ and $U_{\text{epistemic}}^r$ from Eq. \(\ref{eq:epistemic_reward}\)) and minimizes the augmented cost $\cL_{\text{AL}}^c$. The two terms of Eq. \(\ref{eq:al^cost}\) are the standard Lagrangian term $\nu V$ and the quadratic penalty $\frac{\rho_{\text{AL}}}{2} (\max(0, V))^2$, the latter active only when the cost UCB violates the threshold $c_{\text{max}}$.

Dynamic dual updates and prioritized experience replay ensure stable constraint satisfaction throughout training. The dual variable $\lambda$ ascends on the clipped log-ratio of the realized online cost to the budget $c_{\text{max}}$, which is better conditioned than ascending on the violation itself, while the quadratic penalty acts on the cost upper confidence bound, while $\rho_{\text{AL}}$ acts as a fixed penalty coefficient. The coefficients $\lambda_r$ and $\lambda_c$ control the strictness of the uncertainty bounding. Finally, $\alpha$ controls policy entropy, and $\beta$ controls the KL divergence penalty restraining the agent to the IL prior $\pi_{\mathrm{IL}}$. We find that the AL objective significantly stabilizes the dual variable $\nu$ during training compared to standard Lagrangian optimization. We further employ the learned uncertainty estimates to construct a stratified uncertainty prioritized experience replay buffer that balances safety-critical and task-relevant transitions.

Stratified Uncertainty Prioritized Experience Replay (SUPER). We construct a SUPER from the uncertainty estimates of Eqs. \(\ref{eq:aleatoric_cost}\) to \(\ref{eq:epistemic_reward}\). Instead of employing a single priority, we find it beneficial to store two priority values for each transition: one for cost uncertainty ($p^c$) and one for reward uncertainty ($p^r$).

The two priority values for a transition are calculated as the log ratio of epistemic to aleatoric uncertainty for their respective objectives:

\begin{equation} p^c = \log \left( 1 + \frac{U_{\text{epistemic}}^c(\bz, \mathbf{a})}{U_{\text{aleatoric}}^c(\bz, \mathbf{a})} \right) \label{eq:priority_cost} \end{equation}
\begin{equation} p^r = \log \left( 1 + \frac{U_{\text{epistemic}}^r(\bz, \mathbf{a})}{U_{\text{aleatoric}}^r(\bz, \mathbf{a})} \right) \label{eq:priority_reward} \end{equation}

The replay buffer is stratified, maintaining a fixed ratio $\rho_{\text{SUPER}}$ of samples prioritized by cost versus reward. When sampling a batch, a fraction $\rho_{\text{SUPER}}$ of the batch is sampled according to high cost priority ($p^c$), and the remaining $1-\rho_{\text{SUPER}}$ fraction is sampled according to high reward priority ($p^r$). When the buffer is full, new transitions evict the lowest priority samples, based on their respective priority type, to maintain this same $\rho_{\text{SUPER}}$ ratio between the cost and reward strata.

This formulation provides a critical advantage over standard prioritized replay methods. Traditional methods prioritize transitions based on the temporal-difference (TD) error, which cannot distinguish learnable novel states from inherently noisy, unlearnable events, such as unpredictable NPC traffic behavior or sensor noise. By explicitly defining priority as the ratio of epistemic uncertainty (what the model can learn) to aleatoric uncertainty (what the model cannot learn), we actively prevent the buffer from over-sampling noisy transitions that would otherwise waste computational capacity.

Reward Normalization in PPO vs. SAC. We also note several practical differences during training between PPO and SAC. PPO often benefits from reward normalization using a running mean and standard deviation. In contrast, applying the same normalization in SAC significantly degrades performance. In SAC, the critic relies on a stable supervision signal whose magnitude reflects the true reward scale; altering this scale through normalization can therefore destabilize value learning.

S1.4Latent Dynamics Model and Value Expansion

This section gives the full specification of the recurrent latent model introduced in Sec. III-D of the main paper and of the two ways its predictions enter the critic targets. The module is trained on its own loss, receives no RL gradient, and is read by nothing except the target computation, so it can be described independently of everything above.

Recurrent State-Space Model. The model observes the feature stream rather than pixels. Its per-frame observation is $\be_t = [\bz_t, \brho_t] \in \mathbb{R}^{522}$, where $\bz_t$ is the frozen imitation embedding and $\brho_t$ collects the route scalars, i.e., speed, the relative target point, the high-level command one-hot, and the time elapsed since the last full stop. Following DreamerV3 [45], the model factorizes its state into a deterministic recurrent component $\bh_t \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$, produced by a GRU [46], and a stochastic component $\bxi_t$ that is categorical rather than Gaussian, with 16 groups of 16 classes and a $1\%$ uniform mixture folded into every categorical to keep the log probabilities bounded. The belief is their concatenation, $\bb_t = [\bh_t, \bxi_t] \in \mathbb{R}^{512}$. The recurrence, the posterior, and the prior are

\begin{equation} \bh_t = f_\omega(\bh_{t-1}, \bxi_{t-1}, \ba_{t-1}), \; \bxi_t \sim q_\omega(\cdot \mid \bh_t, \be_t), \; \hat{\bxi}_t \sim p_\omega(\cdot \mid \bh_t), \label{eq:rssm_supp} \end{equation}

where $\ba_{t-1}$ is the executed action rather than a resampled one, which matters because the replay buffer is off-policy and the model must describe the dynamics that were actually experienced. Four heads read $[\bb_t, \ba_t]$: a decoder that reconstructs $\be_t$, a reward head regressed in symlog space so that the rare large returns of a terminal event do not dominate the gradient, a Bernoulli cost head, and a Bernoulli termination head. A fifth auxiliary head predicts whether a failure occurs within $m$ steps, supervised at $m \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16\}$ with the labels censored when a sequence ends before the horizon is reached.

Objective. The model is trained on short sequences drawn from the replay buffer, so it sees the same data as the critics and needs no collection machinery of its own, with

\begin{equation} \begin{split} \cL_{\text{WM}}(\omega) = \E \Big[ \; & \| \hat{\be}_t - \be_t \|_2^2 + \big( \hat{r}_t - \mathrm{symlog}(r_t) \big)^2 \\ & + \mathrm{BCE}_{w^{+}}(\hat{c}_t, c_t) + \mathrm{BCE}(\hat{\delta}_t, \delta_t) \\ & + \beta_{\text{dyn}} \max \! \big( \lambda_{\text{free}}, \, \mathrm{KL}[\mathrm{sg}(q_\omega) \| p_\omega] \big) \\ & + \beta_{\text{rep}} \max \! \big( \lambda_{\text{free}}, \, \mathrm{KL}[q_\omega \| \mathrm{sg}(p_\omega)] \big) \\ & + \lambda_{\text{aux}} \cL_{\text{aux}} \; \Big], \end{split} \label{eq:wm_loss_supp} \end{equation}

In Eq. \(\ref{eq:wm_loss_supp}\), $\delta_t$ is the termination indicator, $\mathrm{sg}$ is a stop gradient, and $w^{+}$ is a positive-class weight on the cost head that keeps the rare collision frames from being ignored. The two KL terms are the balanced form of [45]: the first trains the prior toward the posterior and the second regularizes the posterior toward the prior, at different rates, and both are floored at $\lambda_{\text{free}}$ free bits per sample and step so that the model is not driven to a degenerate posterior early in training. The last term is the auxiliary failure-within-$m$ head described above, which is what ties the model's representation to the range of horizons the expansion actually queries rather than to one-step prediction alone.

Recomputing the Recurrent State. To imagine forward from a replayed transition at time $t$ the model needs $\bh_t$, which is not part of the transition. The usual solution is to write the recurrent state into the replay buffer at collection time, where it is read back many updates later, by parameters other than the ones that produced it [47]. We never store it. Instead we unroll Eq. \(\ref{eq:rssm_supp}\) from $\bh = \mathbf{0}$ over the $K_b-1$ frames preceding $t$, taking the mode of $\bxi$ instead of sampling so that the map is deterministic, and masking frames that fall before the start of the episode so that they leave $\bh$ untouched. The belief is then a fixed function of a bounded observation window and of the current parameters $\omega$, so it is always consistent with the model that is about to be rolled forward from it. The cost is storage: each transition carries $(K_b-1)(522+7)$ half-precision values of feature and action history, i.e., 15870 bytes per transition, or roughly 6.35GB at our replay capacity of 400K transitions. With $K_b=16$ at 5Hz the window spans 3.0s of driving. The stored history is a training-time cost only: at run time the agent already holds its own recent frames and advances the same filter online.

Expanded Targets. The critic target is extended rather than replaced. We keep all $n$ real environment steps of the $n$-step return and imagine only past the point where the model-free target would bootstrap. Starting from the real landing observation and its belief, we repeat $H$ times: sample an action from the current policy, query the reward, cost, and termination heads on $[\bb, \ba]$, advance the GRU, sample $\bxi$ from the prior since no observation is available in imagination, and decode $[\hat{\bz}, \hat{\brho}]$ to form the next policy and critic input. The actor is therefore queried in imagination on the same pair it receives in the environment, i.e. an embedding and a belief, with the embedding decoded rather than encoded from an observation. Predicted rewards and expected costs are both accumulated with $\gamma$, multiplied by the running continuation probability $\hat{\Gamma}_h = \prod_{l<h}(1 - \hat{\delta}_{t+n+l})$, and the Bernoulli cost probability is multiplied by the per-event cost mass so that it lands on the same scale as the costs the critic is trained on. At the imagined endpoint we bootstrap through the model-free target path unchanged, i.e., with the same steer smoothing, the same pessimistic or optimistic ensemble subset selection, and the same entropy shift, which gives Eq. 7 of the main paper. The reward and cost targets are read off the same rollout at the same horizon $H$, so the two critics are never regressed toward tails of different length. The cost head is a Bernoulli probability, so its imagined tail is the noisier of the two; we average it over a small number of sampled rollouts per row, which costs a few extra latent steps and leaves the horizon a single number.

Alg. S2 states the model update and the expanded target as a single step, in the order they are interleaved with the main loop of Alg. S1: the model is refreshed on its own cadence, the belief is recomputed for the sampled batch, and one imagination rollout produces both critic targets before the blend is applied. The blend weight of Eq. 8 is the product of two gates. The ramp $\eta_{\text{ramp}}$ rises linearly from zero once the critics are out of warm-up, so they are never handed model-derived targets while they are still stabilizing. The data gate $\eta_{\text{data}}$ scales with how much data the model has consumed, which prevents a restart from assigning a large weight to a model that has seen almost nothing. Both gates are global, so every row of the batch is blended at the same $w$, whichever partition of the stratified buffer of Sec. III-C it was drawn from. Serving the cost-prioritized rows with their own, longer cost-only expansion is the obvious alternative, since those rows are the ones that most need reach, but it is not free: a second rollout at a second horizon is too expensive to run on every batch, so it has to be cached and refreshed asynchronously, which puts tails of unequal age and unequal length into the same critic update. We keep $H$ a single number instead, for both critics and both partitions, and let the sweep in Table III(d) of the main paper choose it.

S1.6Baselines Summary

In this section, we overview several recent baselines and position our approach relative to them. Meaningful comparisons among models can be difficult, as different approaches rely on varying supervision signals and architectural choices, e.g., including temporal inputs, privileged auxiliary tasks, large models, or substantial computational resources. These differences include distinct training datasets and driving experts [18, 7], ensembles [27], and multiple carefully tuned PID controllers; for example, Renz et al. [7] report nearly a 20 DS gain for TCP through controller tuning alone. However, RL can potentially go beyond such tuned experts and models.

Raw2Drive. Our main baseline is Raw2Drive [4], a competitive closed-loop RL-based model on CARLA. However, Raw2Drive uses a reward formulation that underperforms the alternatives in our analysis and requires extensive computational resources to train. Greg is an order of magnitude more sample efficient, while also significantly outperforming Raw2Drive in DS on Bench2Drive with both of our model variants.

Vision-Language Models. Several large, computationally expensive models (e.g., LeapAD [48], SimLingo [7], ReCogDrive [26], AutoVLA [49]) have recently been proposed. We follow the evaluation settings used in the original studies, e.g., Town05 for LeapAD [48] and Bench2Drive for the others. Our model outperforms these large models by a noticeable margin across several aspects, including driving performance across benchmarks as well as training and inference efficiency.

HiP-AD. Finally, HiP-AD [19] is a recently introduced planning-based IL approach with strong performance. However, while HiP-AD does not leverage RL, it relies on extensive architectural design: including explicit temporal and planning modules, supervised by multiple direct auxiliary tasks. Thus, it requires manually tuned control components (we note that the aforementioned planning VLMs do so as well). In contrast, our method learns control directly.

Greg learns the entire pipeline end-to-end, without relying on such explicit tuning for closed-loop inference. As Bench2Drive is becoming increasingly saturated, we further evaluate the generalization ability of our method through a challenging sim-to-real experiment (Sec. S2). Specifically, we evaluate CARLA-trained models on HUGSIM [20]. HUGSIM requires highly reactive maneuvers, particularly under extreme evaluation settings with adversarial agents. This setup helps expose control overfitting, e.g., models that may be carefully tuned for the standard CARLA driving task (slower reaction, wide turning). We also note that HiP-AD leverages several dense auxiliary supervision tasks, which can boost performance but may limit scalability.

Other Baselines. Many promising IL-based variants have been introduced by prior work [50, 51, 19, 52], often with increased modeling complexity. While a complete survey is beyond our scope, we highlight several more recent approaches. Hydra-NeXt [27] reaches 73.86 DS on Bench2Drive but introduces substantial latency and computational overhead; the TCP variant of Greg remains 53$\times$ faster while achieving a higher DS. DriveDPO [53] fine-tunes a driving model with DPO [54] and attains 62.02 DS. Greg is also faster than several recent IL-based models, e.g., ETA [55], whose faster configuration reaches 69.53 DS at 20FPS against 78.11 DS at 100FPS for our TCP variant.

S2Findings Summary and Ablations

Main Findings. With Greg, we explore the potential benefits of efficient reinforcement fine-tuning on vision-based control policies in urban driving. Our key findings are threefold. First, large gains can be made despite minimal architectures: a rear-view input, a hybrid action head, and reinforcement fine-tuning carry TCP-Ctrl from 30.47 to 78.11 DS, improving over most prior methods, including Raw2Drive's 71.36 [4] and several recent planning-based vision-language models, such as ReCogDrive [26] (also 71.36). Second, upgrading the network architecture (DINOv3 backbone, transformer head) leads to a new state-of-the-art of 92.03 DS, outperforming the larger and computationally intensive AutoVLA (78.84) [49] and SimLingo [7] (85.94). We further outperform HiP-AD, a strong imitation-learned model (86.77 DS, with extensive temporal, planning, and auxiliary task tuning). Third, we find that many prior RL algorithms and reward formulations fail to be easily mapped to our settings to produce improvements. We provide additional ablations to analyze these behaviors.

S2.1Training Analysis

Training Components Ablations. The incremental component-wise ablation and the batch-size comparison against PPO and vanilla SAC are reported in Fig. 3 of the main paper. Below we complement them with the reward, buffer, and architecture ablations that isolate each design choice individually.

Reward and Uncertainty Curves. Our approach demonstrates stable convergence and healthy training dynamics over long horizons. Specifically, average reward consistently increases while cost decreases over time. Furthermore, while uncertainty initially increases, it subsequently reduces as the critics learn the environment, facilitating further improvement of the setup. Fig. S1 shows the plots for reward and cost, alongside the corresponding epistemic and aleatoric uncertainties.

S2.2Reward and Component Ablations

We initially perform our ablations using the efficient TCP-based model variant. We then transfer the training settings, as is, to the DINOv3-based model. Unless otherwise stated, results are averaged over three seeds.

Reward Function. The comparison against the reward formulations of CaRL [9], GigaFlow [3], and Raw2Drive [4], as well as against an additive variant of our own reward, is reported in Table III of the main paper. Here we isolate the remaining design choices individually.

Cost Threshold $c_{\text{max}}$ Sensitivity. The cost threshold $c_{\text{max}}$ is not tuned. We set it two standard deviations below the empirical average cost of the initialized $\pi_{\text{IL}}$ policy, $\bar{c}_{\pi_{\mathrm{IL}}} = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_{\mathrm{IL}}}\left[\sum_{t}\gamma^t c(\mathbf{o}_t,\mathbf{a}_t)\right]$, measured over a short warm-up in which the constraint is held slack. The threshold is then strict enough to demand an improvement over $\pi_{\text{IL}}$ and loose enough to stay reachable. Both directions cost performance: leaving the threshold at $\bar{c}_{\pi_{\mathrm{IL}}}$ asks for no improvement at all, while tightening it to $\bar{c}_{\pi_{\mathrm{IL}}}-4\sigma$ puts the constraint out of reach early in training, so the agent spends its updates on constraint satisfaction rather than on driving.

Reward Term Ablation. Table S4 shows that the full reward formulation achieves the strongest performance on Bench2Drive. Termination handling, intrinsic exploration, and the cost term each contribute meaningful improvements in both DS and SR. We further observe that intrinsic exploration substantially improves training efficiency, reducing convergence time by roughly half. The cost term also improves early-stage training and overall DS.

Stratified UPER. While we observe main stability and policy performance gains due to the CMDP formulation, our SUPER pushes the final DS on Bench2Drive: from 55.21 DS to 60.38 DS leveraging TCP-Ctrl and from 71.90 to 75.72 DS with TCP-Ctrl+.

Scalable, Perception-based Reward Ablation. Our uncertainty-based regularization (Sec. S1.3) provides robustness to perception noise. To evaluate this, we replace the CARLA reward signals with outputs from two off-the-shelf YOLO [56] models for detecting lane markings, 2D objects, and traffic signs. To compute the spatial metrics required by our reward functions, we project these 2D camera detections into a 3D bird's-eye-view representation using a homography matrix derived from the known camera extrinsics. While this requires further validation in the future, under this perception-based setup, the final policy DS remains nearly unchanged (within 0.5%), demonstrating that our method operates robustly without requiring privileged simulator information. This result is encouraging for real-world deployment. We further validate this observation through RL experiments on HUGSIM in the next section.

The multi-ability breakdown on Bench2Drive (Table I) shows that Greg matches or exceeds state-of-the-art methods across merging, overtaking, emergency braking, giving way, and traffic sign compliance. Zero-shot transfer to HUGSIM, the BlackOut corruption tiers, and the Agility adversarial scenarios are all in Table II; in each case the reinforcement-trained policies degrade far more gracefully than the imitation-trained ones, including under an architecture-controlled comparison that shares the DINOv3 backbone and transformer head. S3Limitations and Qualitative Results

We discuss additional limitations of our approach below and provide qualitative results in a supplementary video.

Training deep neural policies with RL remains challenging, and our approach does not fully resolve the optimization issues that arise when scaling to larger models or training all layers from scratch. Although we rely on imitation-learning initialization and fine-tune only a subset of layers, more aggressive updates can destabilize the learning process. This suggests that parameter-efficient strategies such as adapter layers or low-rank adaptation require further study in the context of off-policy RL. RL also remains sample inefficient. Fine-tuning from an existing policy reduces the required interaction, but scaling to vast datasets or continuous online learning would still be expensive in real world settings. Safety and deployment constraints introduce additional gaps between simulation and reality, since current RL methods can be unsafe during exploration and remain difficult to certify outside controlled environments. These challenges motivate combining our framework with conservative policy updates, offline datasets, or model-based planning.

Extending our structured objectives to preference-based or learned reward signals remains an open problem. Taken together, these limitations suggest that our method is a step toward practical RL-driven autonomy; however, safe, data-efficient, and robust real-world deployment will require advances in scalable training, safe RL, and realistic reward design. For transparency, we include examples of policy failure cases in Sec. S3.

While our method learns a direct sensorimotor control policy and achieves competitive or superior performance with a significantly simpler pipeline (e.g., Raw2Drive [4], SimLingo [7]), several limitations remain. Our approach assumes access to sensory observations, high-level navigation plans, and basic traffic-violation signals. Here, high-level plans can be obtained without HD maps [64], e.g., through standard navigation services such as Google Maps [65] or OpenStreetMap [16, 66]. Vehicle speed and control measurements are available from the CAN bus. However, other reward signals, such as lane-keeping and traffic-light compliance, may be noisy to obtain in the real-world from camera perception. A larger-scale study could help further validate the robustness of our approach to perception errors in the future. Moreover, although we explore various initializations to our model, our method assumes access to an offline driving dataset. This requirement can be obtained from several publicly available datasets [67, 68].

Qualitative Examples and Failure Cases. Figs. S2, S3, and S4 depict Grad-CAM [69] attention visualizations for the TCP variant. Figs. S5, S6, S7, S8, and S9 show Grad-CAM for our DINOv3 variant.

Our policy still exhibits several characteristic failure modes on Bench2Drive. Fig. S10 illustrates representative examples, including incorrect steering during left turns, collisions with pedestrians, unsafe lane changes into oncoming traffic, getting stuck behind static obstacles, and side collisions during sequential lane changes.
